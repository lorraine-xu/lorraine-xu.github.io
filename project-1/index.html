Project 1 · Images of the Russian Empire

Putting the Prokudin-Gorskii Plates Back in Color

Aligning the red, green, and blue glass plate scans of Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii into single color photos, using exhaustive search, an image pyramid, and a handful of extra tricks to clean up the result.

Lorraine Xu · lorraine_xu@berkeley.edu

00

Overview

Between 1907 and 1915, Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in color, years before color film existed. His trick was to take three black and white exposures of the same scene through a red, a green, and a blue filter, one after another, on a single tall glass plate. Stack the three exposures back together and you get a color photo, as long as they line up.

They almost never line up perfectly. The camera moved slightly between exposures, or the plate shifted, so the three channels need to be nudged back into place by hand, or in this case, by code. This page walks through how I did that: a simple brute force search for the small images, a coarse to fine pyramid for the huge full resolution scans, and five extra techniques to make the final images look better than a plain stack of channels.

01

Single-Scale Alignment

The idea is simple: try every small shift of the green and red channels against the blue channel, score how well each shift lines them up, and keep the best one. I tried shifts from -15 to 15 pixels in both directions, which is 31 by 31 = 961 candidates per channel. I scored each candidate two ways: the L2 distance (just the raw pixel difference, lower is better) and normalized cross-correlation, or NCC (how well the two images vary together after removing their own brightness, higher is better).

One thing that mattered more than I expected: I had to crop out a margin before scoring each candidate. Shifting an image with np.roll wraps pixels around from the opposite edge, and comparing that wrapped edge to the real photo can quietly throw off which shift looks best. Cutting off a margin equal to the search window before computing the score fixed this.

All offsets are written as (x, y), meaning (horizontal shift, vertical shift), and all three came out crisp with no ghosting.

On this run, L2 and NCC agreed on the offsets for all three images. That is not always true on the harder full resolution scans later on, where the two metrics sometimes land on slightly different shifts, which I talk about in the bells and whistles section.

02

Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment

Brute force search works fine on a small jpg, but it falls apart on the full resolution tif scans. Those images are much bigger, and the true offset between channels can be much larger too, sometimes over a hundred pixels. Searching a wide enough window at full resolution would take far too long.

The fix is a pyramid. I shrink both images by half repeatedly, using a simple 2 by 2 block average (no library resize function), until the image is small enough to brute force search cheaply. I find the best shift there, double it to convert it back to the next resolution up, and then run a small brute force search around that estimate to correct any rounding error from the doubling. I repeat this on the way back up to full resolution. This turns an expensive search into a handful of cheap ones, and every image below finished in under 5 seconds.

Here are all 17 images (14 provided, plus 3 of my own from the Library of Congress collection), aligned with the pyramid. All of them came out aligned with no visible ghosting.

* emir and ilemselga needed one extra trick to align well: aligning on image gradients instead of raw pixels. More on that in the bells and whistles section below, since it explains why these two are marked.

On failures. The assignment allows up to 1 of the 17 images to come out misaligned. After the gradient fix for emir and ilemselga, all 17 of my images look aligned with no visible ghosting. I do not have a failure case to report here, which is a nicer problem to have than the alternative, but it does mean I do not have a "why it failed" story for this part.
03

Bells and Whistles

The plain aligned photos still have a few problems: some images need a different way of measuring alignment, most look a little flat, some have a color cast, and almost all of them have a colorful border left over from shifting the channels. Here are five things I tried to fix that.

  • Better features
  • Auto contrast
  • Auto white balance
  • Auto crop
  • Better color mapping

Better features

emir.tif is a known hard case for this assignment: the blue channel and the other two channels do not share the same brightness, so comparing raw pixels can point the search toward the wrong shift. Even after widening my search window, aligning on raw pixels still left visible ghosting once you zoom in on any fine detail, like the fabric pattern and the belt he is wearing below.

The fix: instead of comparing raw pixel values, I compute a gradient magnitude map for each channel first (how much the brightness changes at each pixel, using np.gradient), and align on those maps instead. Edges tend to line up regardless of each channel's absolute brightness, so this sidesteps the problem. I still apply the resulting shift to the original color pixels, the gradient maps are only used to find where to shift.

The same fix cleaned up ilemselga.tif as well.

Auto contrast

Many of the aligned photos do not use the full range of brightness values, so they look a little washed out. The fix is to stretch intensities so a dark percentile maps to 0 and a bright percentile maps to 1 (I use percentiles instead of the literal darkest and brightest pixel, so one dust speck or scratch does not set the whole scale).

My first attempt stretched all three channels together using one shared range. It barely changed anything, because most photos already touch close to black and white somewhere, even if the bulk of the image looks flat. Stretching each channel on its own range, using a wider percentile cutoff (5th to 95th instead of 0.5th to 99.5th), made a real difference.

Auto white balance

Some photos, like church.tif, have an obvious color cast: the sky, water, and even the white church building all looked slightly purple. Measuring the average color of each channel showed why: blue averaged 0.72, while red and green averaged around 0.51, a real and consistent bias, not just an unlucky pixel.

I tried two fixes. Gray world assumes the average color in the photo should be neutral gray, so it scales each channel to bring its mean in line with the others. White patch assumes the brightest pixels should be white, so it scales channels to match at the high end. Gray world worked well here. White patch barely changed anything, because the brightest pixels in this photo are already-overexposed clouds, which are already close to white in every channel, so there was nothing there for white patch to fix.

Auto crop

Shifting channels with np.roll wraps a strip of pixels around from the opposite edge, and the plates themselves often have their own white or black borders too, so almost every aligned photo has a colorful strip around the edge. Instead of cropping a fixed margin, I scan inward from each edge and look for where the local variation in brightness jumps up, assuming a plain border has low variation and real photo content has more texture.

This worked cleanly on the left and right edges, which really are close to one solid color for a stretch before the real photo starts. It worked much less well on the top and bottom edges. On monastery.jpg, the very top row already has too much variation for a simple threshold to treat it as "still border," since the sky right at the edge already has some texture from clouds. Looking closer, the border on this photo is not even one color, it is a white strip, then a black strip, then a colorful blend, before you reach the real sky. Switching from a fixed brightness threshold to looking for the single biggest jump in variation helped a little on every edge, but did not fully solve it.

I'm keeping this result and being upfront about the limit here, rather than tuning the threshold by hand until this one photo looks clean. A proper fix would need to notice a long run of low variation, not just the single biggest jump, which is a reasonable next step if I revisit this.

Better color mapping

There is no reason the red, green, and blue filters Prokudin-Gorskii used should line up exactly with modern RGB. I tried histogram matching: instead of just matching each channel's average brightness like white balance does, this matches the full distribution of pixel values in red and green onto blue's distribution.

On church.tif this made the picture brighter overall, not more natural, because blue was already the unusually bright channel to begin with, so matching red and green onto it just pulled everything up to blue's level. A fairer version would match all three channels to some compromise target instead of picking one real channel as the answer. I'm noting this as a real limitation of the simple version rather than pretending it worked better than it did.

04

Version Notes

Two changes along the way were worth keeping a record of, since both came from a real bug or a real limitation I ran into, not just tuning for its own sake.

Single-scale: adding a scoring margin

Early on, my single-scale search scored every candidate shift on the full image, wrapped edges included. I added margin = window + 5 and scored only the cropped interior, shifted[margin:-margin, margin:-margin] against the same crop of the other channel. This changed the chosen offset on cathedral.jpg and monastery.jpg (about 19 out of 255 in average pixel value between the two versions), and left tobolsk.jpg unchanged, since its correct answer did not happen to depend on the wrapped edge.

Pyramid: widening the window and raising min_size

I moved the search window from 15 to 30 pixels, and the pyramid's stopping size (how small an image gets before switching to brute force) from 200 to 400 pixels. This was a direct response to church.tif, which came out badly misaligned at the old settings: the true shift was large enough, and the coarsest level was blurred enough after extra rounds of downsampling, that the search settled on the wrong answer well before it even reached full resolution. The new settings fixed church.tif and changed tobolsk.jpg's result too, since a smaller min_size used to force even that small jpg through one unnecessary round of the pyramid; at 400, it now gets solved directly with a single, wider brute force search instead. cathedral.jpg and monastery.jpg came out identical either way.
